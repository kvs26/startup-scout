# Explain a business the reader can picture

Read this **before drafting** an opportunity summary, a comparison's idea explanations or the final explanation in chat. Apply the same plain-language standard to explanations inside research files; their detailed evidence can remain there. Assume the reader has never encountered the activity or product format. A draft is ready only when they can reconstruct one actual use and explain what they would buy from the text alone.

## Build the explanation around a customer situation

Lead with a particular customer situation that makes the business question matter. Show what the customer wants to do or experience and what we would sell. A business may address a work difficulty, improve an existing paid choice or provide enjoyment, convenience, identity or companionship. Explain the actual buying motive without inventing an operational problem. Use people and actions: who sends, checks, approves, receives and pays. Make clear whether the offer is software the customer operates, a service we deliver with software, or another business model. Describe the proposed first version; distinguish optional later additions.

Walk through one concrete use before discussing market evidence. Explain the basic rules or workflow first, including who operates what and what the other people do. Then show the starting input, the actual contents the customer encounters, their next action and how the use ends. If the product displays text, a picture, a chart, a message or a file, give a small literal example of its contents. “An absurd diagram appears” leaves the crucial action unexplained; name what is drawn and how the person responds. A setting and a colorful premise alone do not make a worked example.

Separate customer use from product creation. Explain which software, person or service performs each consequential action. For an unfamiliar game, the reader should understand who sees the material first, what the player must do, what the host does and when the turn ends. For unfamiliar work, explain the equivalent handoffs and usable result. These are explanation requirements, not extra product features to invent.

State who would pay, what the payment buys, and the charging basis: per project, license, month, transaction or another relevant unit. Explain price and payment terms to the extent known. A fee with an unset price can still be explained clearly. Label a proposed payer or model as a proposal when evidence is missing.

Write the business explanation in connected prose first; put record metadata and research findings afterward. Preserve any required up-front source-access status, but keep it brief. Let each new point answer a question raised by the situation before it. A concrete detail should explain an action or decision. Adapt the prose and length to the idea; cutting the steps that make it understandable is not concision.

## Make unfamiliar language earn its place

Use words you would say to this reader in conversation. Replace abstract phrases with the people, objects and actions they represent. Prefer “a short slide presentation” to an unexplained “deck,” “opens in a browser without an internet connection” to “offline player,” and an explanation of the actual work to “bounded delivery.” Define a necessary domain term before relying on it. Build each sentence on something the previous sentence has introduced; supply missing steps instead of shortening a confusing explanation further.

For software, show what the user gives it, what it does and what the user does with the result. For a service, show the provider's work and the deliverable. For a marketplace, identify both sides and the transaction. These are ways to reveal the business, not reasons to change the supplied idea.

## Keep the explanation honest

Label invented people, inputs, counts and outcomes as an **imagined example**. Use them to illustrate the proposed workflow. Keep observed customer behavior and source-backed results in the findings, with evidence links. Hypothetical payments, saved time and successful outcomes remain assumptions.

After the explanation, state the strongest support, the most relevant existing alternative, and the biggest business doubt. Preserve qualifications that change the decision. If the offer itself is unresolved, name the specific choice; clarity does not require inventing a price, demand, advantage or delivery capability. The summary should remain understandable without opening its research links.

## Keep the summary useful for a decision

Explain each point once. The opening and example can already answer who the customer is, what the first version does and what payment buys; do not repeat those answers under three more headings. Use the [summary template](templates/opportunity-summary.md) as an adaptable outline, not a form whose every heading needs prose.

Keep the main evidence, strongest alternative, decisive doubt, founder responsibilities, cost/budget implication and next decision on the summary page. Put the source inventory, repeated qualifications, line-by-line fee calculations and additional financial scenarios in `research.md` or an existing economics file. Summarize their implication in everyday language: a small positive amount per sale still has to cover marketing and founder time, or payment may arrive well after the first order. A material unknown must remain visible even if its detailed calculation moves. Do not replace the explanation with labels such as “moderate confidence” or a wall of caveats.

For this founder, explain how AI would create the actual sellable material and what the founder would direct, check or handle personally. Follow the [production assessment](research-guide.md#assess-the-founders-production-workflow). “AI drafts, founder edits” is incomplete when that hides the main creative work. State whether corrections can be directed back to AI or require manual production/expert judgment that has not been established. A short software build estimate cannot stand in for producing enjoyable, accurate or reliable content.

## Worked example: an unfamiliar party game

> We would sell a downloadable game for friends at a gathering. One person gives a pretend serious presentation using slides they have never seen. The host shows the slides one at a time on a television; the player has to invent an explanation as each appears. The surprise is the activity: the player is trying to connect the unexpected slides into a story.
>
> **Imagined example:** Riya volunteers to speak. The first slide says, “My fridge runs a nightclub.” She begins, “The party starts when we switch off the kitchen lights.” The host advances to a picture of milk cartons queuing outside a fridge, with a cucumber guarding the door. Riya now has to explain why the cucumber is the bouncer. A third slide shows a chart where cheese is the most popular dance partner. She invents a reason and finishes her story. After about three minutes, another friend takes a turn with a different unseen presentation.
>
> The proposed buyer is the host. A suggested $7 payment would buy twelve ready-made presentations and instructions, which the host downloads before the gathering. The proposed format and price have not been tested. The example above illustrates how the game would work; it is not finished game content or evidence that people find it funny.

This supplies observable content and actions; it does not establish production quality or demand. If AI is meant to create these presentations, the assessment must still explain how a coherent, playable sequence would be generated, corrected and checked with ordinary players.

## Worked example: family letter editions

The earlier summary led with “a checked digital edition,” a “full transcript,” and “source-page references.” Those terms named parts of the output without showing the paid work.

An explanation of the same proposal:

> We would offer families a paid service that turns scans of old handwritten letters into a checked, typed collection they can read and share. Software produces a first draft; someone who can read the handwriting checks it against the original pages.
>
> **Imagined example:** Meera has scanned 100 pages of her grandmother's letters. She wants her relatives to read them without each person deciphering the handwriting. She sends the scans, agrees the scope after we check a few pages, and receives the typed letters with unclear words marked, notes identifying the original scan for each letter, and a list of names and where they appear. If she also wants a shorter collection of selected letters, she approves what goes into it.
>
> The proposed customer is the collection's owner, paying once for the agreed project. The fee would need to cover software, proofreading and preparation. A price has not been established. Existing software and professional services already do similar work; whether an owner would choose and pay a new provider remains unanswered.

The name, page count and sequence illustrate the offer; they are not a customer result or a measured workload. A clear explanation makes this proposal easier to evaluate, while its weak demand and cost evidence remain visible.

## Check understanding before saving

Before saving or presenting the draft, read only the explanation, without its research, prompt or links. Write a brief retelling in working notes using only facts present in the draft:

- Who is the customer, and what situation creates the need?
- What exactly do they see/provide, what happens next, who acts and how does one use end? Point to the literal input/output/example in the draft.
- What is being sold, who would pay, and what does one payment cover?
- How is this task handled now, and what reason to choose the proposed offer is supported or still unproven?
- Which details are observed, proposed, imagined or unknown?
- How would the sellable output be produced, what must this founder do, and which quality or timing assumptions remain untested?

Treat a missing answer, an unexplained term, a vague placeholder for the central action or an answer imported from background knowledge as a **failed draft**. Rewrite before saving or recommending; moving that missing explanation behind a link does not repair it. Check that the first several paragraphs explain the experience before asking the reader to weigh research qualifications. Keep the retelling/check in working notes, not as extra boilerplate in the user's summary.

For an unfamiliar format, a previously confusing explanation, or a user-requested readability repair, use a separate reader subagent when available: give it only the proposed explanation and the questions above, without the source research, writer's rationale or intended answers. Have it retell the experience and identify details it had to infer; repair those gaps and recheck changed passages. The reader's output, not its bare “clear” verdict, is the check. If no separate reader is available, do the same pass yourself and record that limit. This tests the written explanation; it neither proves customer comprehension nor validates the business.
