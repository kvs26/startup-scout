# Explain a business the reader can picture

Read this when writing an opportunity summary, a comparison's idea explanations, or the final explanation in chat. Assume the reader is new to the customer's work. The goal is that they can explain what the customer would buy after reading this page alone.

## Build the explanation around a real task

Lead with who the customer is, what they are trying to do, and what we would sell to help. Use people and actions: who sends, checks, approves, receives and pays. Make clear whether the offer is software the customer operates, a service we deliver with software, or another business model. Describe the proposed first version; distinguish optional later additions.

Walk through one concrete use before discussing market evidence. Start with the customer's situation and input, show the important actions, and end with a specific output they can use. Explain what changes for the customer. For example, name the file's contents rather than merely promising an “edition.” Separate automated work, human work and customer decisions where they affect delivery or value.

State who would pay, what the payment buys, and the charging basis: per project, license, month, transaction or another relevant unit. Explain price and payment terms to the extent known. A fee with an unset price can still be explained clearly. Label a proposed payer or model as a proposal when evidence is missing.

Write the business explanation first; put record metadata and research findings afterward. Adapt the prose and length to the idea. A table is useful for comparing ideas after the reader understands them.

## Make unfamiliar language earn its place

Use words you would say to this reader in conversation. Replace abstract phrases with the people, objects and actions they represent. When a domain term matters, explain it on first use: “a transcript, meaning a typed copy of the handwritten letters.” Build each sentence on something the previous sentence has introduced; supply missing steps instead of shortening a confusing explanation further.

For software, show what the user gives it, what it does and what the user does with the result. For a service, show the provider's work and the deliverable. For a marketplace, identify both sides and the transaction. These are ways to reveal the business, not reasons to change the supplied idea.

## Keep the explanation honest

Label invented people, inputs, counts and outcomes as an **imagined example**. Use them to illustrate the proposed workflow. Keep observed customer behavior and source-backed results in the findings, with evidence links. Hypothetical payments, saved time and successful outcomes remain assumptions.

After the explanation, state the strongest support, the most relevant existing alternative, and the biggest business doubt. Preserve qualifications that change the decision. If the offer itself is unresolved, name the specific choice; clarity does not require inventing a price, demand, advantage or delivery capability. The summary should remain understandable without opening its research links.

## Worked example: family letter editions

The earlier summary led with “a checked digital edition,” a “full transcript,” and “source-page references.” Those terms named parts of the output without showing the paid work.

An explanation of the same proposal:

> We would offer families a paid service that turns scans of old handwritten letters into a checked, typed collection they can read and share. Software produces a first draft; someone who can read the handwriting checks it against the original pages.
>
> **Imagined example:** Meera has scanned 100 pages of her grandmother's letters. She wants her relatives to read them without each person deciphering the handwriting. She sends the scans, agrees the scope after we check a few pages, and receives the typed letters with unclear words marked, notes identifying the original scan for each letter, and a list of names and where they appear. If she also wants a shorter collection of selected letters, she approves what goes into it.
>
> The proposed customer is the collection's owner, paying once for the agreed project. The fee would need to cover software, proofreading and preparation. A price has not been established. Existing software and professional services already do similar work; whether an owner would choose and pay a new provider remains unanswered.

The name, page count and sequence illustrate the offer; they are not a customer result or a measured workload. A clear explanation makes this proposal easier to evaluate, while its weak demand and cost evidence remain visible.

## Check understanding before saving

Read the explanation without following links. Can you answer these questions using only what is written?

- Who is the customer, and what situation creates the need?
- What do they provide or do, what does the product or provider do, and what do they receive?
- What is being sold, who would pay, and what does one payment cover?
- How is this task handled now, and what reason to choose the proposed offer is supported or still unproven?
- Which details are observed, proposed, imagined or unknown?

Revise any answer that depends on unexplained terminology, a missing step or unstated background. Read the revision aloud to catch phrases you would not use in conversation. This is a comprehension check, not proof that the business works or that a reader has understood it.
