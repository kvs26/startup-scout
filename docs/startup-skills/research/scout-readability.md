# Making scout summaries easier to read

Research note, 15 October 2026. This is guidance for revising the [fictional agency invoice example](../../../.scratch/startup-skills/prototypes/scout-output/opportunities/agency-invoice-follow-up/summary.md) and the scout's writing instructions, not an assessment of whether the business idea is real.

## What the sources suggest

1. **Follow the reader's question.** In a [first-person interview](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20030407/37969-a-different-kind-of-journey.html), Bill Bryson says he began *A Short History of Nearly Everything* by asking what made science interesting and kept asking experts to explain things again. He also describes using human stories when the science gets hard. **Application:** Open with the agency owner's task and why it is frustrating. Then explain what changed the scout's view. This is an inference from Bryson's method, not a claim that his prose style can be reduced to a formula.
2. **Make the unfamiliar visible.** In a [publisher-authorized extract from *The Body*](https://www.penguin.co.nz/books/the-body-9780552779906/extracts/2161-the-body), Bryson anchors an abstract question about chemical elements in one recognizable person, a price, familiar experiences with heavy water, and a surprising limit to the comparison: buying the elements does not make a living person. **Application:** Say what someone actually does with an invoice, reminder, or dispute before naming the proposed category. A concrete example must illustrate the evidence accurately; it cannot replace evidence.
3. **Give each new point a foothold.** Clark and Haviland's [original account of the “given-new contract”](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Clark%2C%20H.H.%20_%20Haviland%2C%20S.E.%20_Comprehension%20and%20the%20given-new%20contract_%201977.pdf) explains that listeners connect new information to something already established. The authors review linguistic and psychological evidence for extra effort when the connection is missing. In [experiments on science texts](https://experts.azregents.edu/en/publications/are-good-texts-always-better-interactions-of-text-coherence-backg/), McNamara and colleagues found that readers with little background knowledge benefited from more coherent, explanatory text. **Application:** Introduce the owner and the late invoice, then the existing reminder tool, then why a dispute may still need human follow-up. State the causal bridge instead of making readers infer it from E1–E4 labels. The science-text results are about comprehension, not a proven template for business writing.
4. **Use the words the situation needs.** In [a series of experiments](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.1178), Oppenheimer found that needlessly complex wording lowered readers' judgments of an author's intelligence, with processing fluency implicated. Grice's [original discussion of cooperative communication](https://web.stanford.edu/class/psych205/papers/Grice-1975.pdf) treats adequate information, truthfulness, relevance, and clarity as expectations in conversation. **Application:** Replace phrases such as “incumbent feature,” “possible payer,” and “current feature recheck” with the person, product, and action meant. Keep necessary terms such as “disputed invoice”; define them in context. Oppenheimer measured judgments in experimental settings, not every dimension of good prose.
5. **Let the story serve the point.** Bryson's example shows how human interest can make a hard topic approachable, but interest alone is not the test. In [four experiments with explanatory science passages](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Richard-Mayer-4/publication/232595492_How_Seductive_Details_Do_Their_Damage_A_Theory_of_Cognitive_Interest_in_Science_Learning/links/57799b8408ae4645d611f204/How-Seductive-Details-Do-Their-Damage-A-Theory-of-Cognitive-Interest-in-Science-Learning.pdf), Harp and Mayer found that entertaining details unrelated to the lesson reduced recall of central ideas and problem-solving transfer. **Application:** Use one relevant customer moment to explain the business question. Avoid invented color, decorative analogies, or humor that imply stronger evidence than the fictional fixture contains. This classroom result is a caution, not a ban on engaging writing.

## An editorial test for the scout

After reading the opening explanation, a person should be able to say **who has the problem, what happens, what the proposed product would do, and why an existing tool may fall short**. Then the summary can give the recommendation and a short, plain-language account of the evidence and uncertainty. Put source IDs and scenario-day history behind links unless they alter the decision. Explain delivery assumptions that affect the decision in ordinary words. Keep the **fictional prototype** label visible before any customer claim; preserve the uncertainty about demand, purchase, alternatives, and cost.

The original prototype summary was careful about those uncertainties. Its readability problem was the sequence and vocabulary: it began with metadata, compressed the customer problem into labels, and asked readers to reconstruct the change from “E1–E4” and “day-3 recheck.” These observations are a close reading of that earlier draft, not findings from the cited studies. The [revised example](../../../.scratch/startup-skills/prototypes/scout-output/opportunities/agency-invoice-follow-up/summary.md) puts the customer situation first.
