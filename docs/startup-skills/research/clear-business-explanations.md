# Explaining an unfamiliar business clearly

Research date: 2026-10-11  
Question: How should startup scouting explain a proposed business so a reader unfamiliar with its industry can understand what a customer buys?

## Finding

The writers examined recommend ordinary words, visible people and actions, enough context to follow the reasoning, and deliberate revision. Their advice suggests that replacing jargon alone will not repair an explanation that leaves the reader to infer the customer experience. These are original essays and a published talk, not controlled comparisons of startup-summary formats. The business-specific recommendations below are our synthesis.

The [family-letter summary before the rewrite](../../../opportunities/family-letter-editions/rounds/2026-10-11-before-clarity-rewrite.md) named a service, an imagined customer and a project fee. But terms such as “checked digital edition,” “source-page references,” “editorial approach” and “underlying archive” assumed knowledge of the finished product. Its illustration still ended in an “indexed edition.” The reader had to work out who checks what, what arrives after payment and how it differs from a tool they operate themselves. This is an assessment of that explanation, not a new assessment of customer demand.

## Original sources and their limits

| William Zinsser, [Writing English as a Second Language](https://theamericanscholar.org/writing-english-as-a-second-language/) (talk delivered 2009-08-11; published 2009-12-01) | Readers need specific people doing things they can picture. ## Our synthesis for startup explanations

Use a connected explanation that lets the reader follow a transaction. Adapt its order to the business; this is a writing approach, not a requirement for identical headings or sentence counts.

1. **Orient the reader.** Say what kind of business is proposed, whom it serves and what it helps them accomplish. Name any unusual context in familiar words before adding a specialist term.
2. **Make the difficulty visible.** Describe the situation, what the customer currently does and why that work is troublesome. A reader should recognize the need before encountering a feature list.
3. **Follow the work.** Show what the customer provides, what the business does and what the customer receives. Name the people responsible where it changes the offer. Explain software's role without implying it eliminates checking or specialist work.
4. **Explain the purchase.** Identify the payer, what they pay for and whether payment is per project, per use, a subscription or another model. An unset amount or payment schedule remains unset; clarity does not require inventing a price.
5. **Use an example to teach the mechanics.** Make the starting material, actions and resulting object concrete. Keep the same business model in the example. Label an invented illustration near the example itself; use a sourced account only for what it actually reports.
6. **Then assess the opportunity.** Explain existing alternatives, why this offer might be chosen, what research supports, what challenges it and what could change the recommendation. An appealing story is not evidence that anyone will buy.

Define a technical term through its practical meaning: a transcript is a typed copy of the handwritten words; source references show which original page a passage came from; an index helps a family find a person or place. Keep a term when its precision matters, but do not ask the reader to infer its meaning from another unexplained term.

## Illustration of the proposed change

**Imagined example, not a customer observation:** A family has boxes of handwritten letters from a grandparent. They want relatives to read them, but typing and checking every letter would take substantial work. They send scans of the pages. The proposed business uses existing software to create a first typed version, then a person who can read the handwriting checks it against the scans. Words that cannot be read confidently stay marked as uncertain.

The family receives the typed collection, the original page images and a way to find names and places. References connect the typed passages to their original pages. If the family wants a shorter collection for a book, it approves which letters or passages to include, while retaining the full typed collection. The proposed sale is a fee for completing this project after reviewing a sample. The price and payment schedule have not been decided. This illustration draws only on the [recorded proposition](../../../opportunities/family-letter-editions/research.md#c002) and [delivery and payment proposal](../../../opportunities/family-letter-editions/research.md#c003); it supplies no new demand evidence.

## Recommended skill change and review

Put this explanation approach in a shared writing guide used by the scout and its summary/comparison templates. Have the scout check the explanation before saving or recommending an opportunity: can a reader identify the customer, starting situation, work performed, deliverable, payer and payment model from that page alone? If a reviewer needs another research file to explain what is sold, revise the explanation.

Use a fresh retelling or read-aloud pass to catch abstract wording, then check whether each sentence gives the reader the context needed for the next. Judge missing meaning directly. Word counts, sentence-length targets and readability scores cannot tell whether a reader understands the offer. Actual feedback from the user remains stronger evidence of comprehension than an agent declaring its own draft clear.

Retain the repo's [evidence distinctions](../research-guide.md) when simplifying: label illustrations and assumptions, preserve important uncertainty and contrary findings, and keep citations attached to the claims they support. Clear writing should make those limits easier to understand.
