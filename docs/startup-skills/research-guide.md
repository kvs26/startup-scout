# Research for startup decisions

Use this guide to decide what a finding supports, which questions deserve more research, and what still needs customer evidence. ## From a source to a claim

An attributable customer account, positive or negative, supports a reported experience in its original context. Read the account, date, relevant replies, suggested workarounds and resolutions. Keep geography, customer type and product/version limits. Several articles or list entries based on the same incident remain one underlying observation. Search snippets help locate evidence; inspect the underlying source before relying on its detail, or mark the claim as unverified.

Different sources answer different questions:

| Source | What it can support | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original customer account or supplied result | A reported problem, behavior or outcome in context | Identity/accuracy where unverified, prevalence, relevance to the proposed offer |
| Vendor documentation and pricing | A documented capability, published offer or supplier input | Actual suitability, total costs, satisfaction and willingness to buy a new offer |
| Buyer-authored procurement record | A stated requirement or awarded contract, according to its stage | Supplier eligibility, accessibility and applicability outside that buyer |
| Official statistics | The measured population and quantities in the stated period | Reachable buyers and demand for this particular product |
| Idea list, investor prompt or method advice | A lead, useful terminology or research method | Every opportunity-specific problem and commercial claim |

Make each important claim traceable to supporting and contrary evidence and explain confidence in that claim. A well-supported negative finding can justify revision. Sparse coverage leaves uncertainty; source failure, a search with no relevant results, and a search not performed are distinct states. Avoid extrapolating between markets or customer groups without explaining the assumption.

## Follow-up that changes a decision

Discovery can start from customer tasks, desired experiences or existing spending, including audiences outside startup circles. Use the relevant market's local names and languages when useful. Follow the original source from any curated lead, and search beyond lists for other problems. Explain meaningful coverage gaps such as private communities or a digitally underrepresented customer group.

For promising ideas, investigate both the proposed opportunity and plausible explanations against it: an existing feature, a solved configuration issue, a cheap manual workaround, organizational causes, switching costs or satisfied users. A vendor already offering a feature challenges the claim that it is missing; it does not prove that every possible business in that market is unattractive. An old unresolved thread calls for checking the current product and linked issue/release history.

Prioritize questions whose answers could change the next step. For example, if a complaint assumes offline use is impossible, follow product documentation and relevant practitioner replies before recommending a new offline product. If the answer depends on private usage or purchasing decisions, identify the missing customer observation instead of treating more web pages as a substitute.

Scouting needs enough depth to compare the customer motive, alternatives, reason to choose the offer, payer/revenue possibilities, acquisition routes, initial delivery/support effort, material costs and dependencies. Investigation goes deeper into offer/payment terms, buying authority/process, acquisition, onboarding, continuing value, costs/cash timing and technical/data access. Apply questions to the business model; one-time licenses, usage fees, marketplaces and services enabled by software need not use subscription measures.

## Creator material and attention signals

Choose relevant YouTube/Instagram originals and source-owned text according to the question and audience. The [source catalog](source-catalog.md#creator-resources) records channel candidates and actual access states. For scouting, the [source-readiness check](../../.agents/skills/startup-idea-scout/references/source-readiness.md) determines required coverage and when to pause; a catalog preference never overrides the brief or a supplied reference. A video title, description or episode synopsis locates a lead; it does not establish the spoken content. Distinguish creator-written articles, publisher transcripts, YouTube captions, audio and visual inspection. Inspect relevant frames when a demonstration or diagram matters; text alone may omit decisive context.

For supplied transcripts/screenshots/copied text, retain the original URL, speaker/author, asserted publication date and timestamps, receipt date, supplied-by-user status, omissions and what was actually inspected. Attribute uncertain origins; do not label supplied excerpts independently fetched or watched. If missing material could change a decision, disclose it immediately and give the original link and precise passage/frame request, with timestamps only if known. In scouting, follow the source-readiness pause rule before candidate research. Only optional coverage gaps permit routine continuation; an essential unsupported claim keeps the affected conclusion provisional.

Treat creator/guest/seller earnings as attributed reports. Preserve currency, period and whether the number means revenue, profit, gross transaction volume or payout. Note sponsorship, affiliate links, courses, communities or portfolio interests where visible. Distinguish earnings from selling a business's product/service from earnings teaching others how to do it. Follow material claims to original operator/customer evidence and current costs; popularity or a screenshot alone cannot establish our economics.

Deduplicate the original business/event across videos, reels, newsletters, interviews and copied articles. A website plus its founder's channel is not automatically independent corroboration. Keep separately observed events distinct, with their dates and limits.

For an emerging-attention hypothesis, compare dated originals with earlier material and channel context. A cumulative view count is attention, not a growth rate; growth claims need repeated comparable observations. Separate curiosity from purchases, use and repeat value. Propose a concrete audience/channel, sharing hook or demonstration, effort/cash budget, measurement period and a test connecting attention to relevant visits, activation and payment. Include modest reach, conversion uncertainty, costs and fading-interest cases. Virality remains uncertain; proposing an experiment does not run it. The scout's [commercial guide](../../.agents/skills/startup-idea-scout/references/commercial-assessment.md#small-products-and-speed-to-revenue) owns how this affects quick-product comparison.

## Numbers and delivery requirements

Distinguish sourced, user-supplied and hypothetical inputs. Published competitor prices are evidence about that competitor, not the price a new customer will accept. Record currency, market, date, units, billing period, plan limits and tax treatment when material. Refresh changing inputs for the decision being made. Retain original amounts and any conversion basis.

Calculations show the formula, inputs and assumptions. Useful ranges or scenarios are preferable when uncertainty warrants them; avoid invented precision or an unsupported profit ranking. Simulated revenue is not a forecast. Include founder work, startup and recurring spend, usage-driven supplier costs, customer acquisition/support and timing where they affect the decision. An unpriced dependency remains unpriced.

Solo status makes installation, field work, specialist knowledge, integrations, capital and support obligations important facts. Explain what must be done and which estimates are assumptions. Do not silently turn those requirements into an eligibility filter or claim the founder can immediately deliver alone.

## Assess the founder's production workflow

Read the founder profile and assess the way this founder intends to produce the offer. Their baseline is AI creation through separate skills/automations, with personal direction, decisions and customer conversations. AI used to build the offer need not be a feature sold to customers. Lack of manual coding, drawing or writing experience alone does not disqualify an idea; access to AI alone does not establish paid-delivery quality. This assessment plans or evaluates production; it does not create the product.

For each material part of the sellable output, explain:

- **Creation:** the actual input, AI task and output, plus a plausible tool/capability route and whether it is documented, assumed or demonstrated. Include the content or creative assets that give the product value, not just its software shell.
- **Direction and correction:** what the founder specifies, selects and checks, how feedback returns to AI, and what happens when it fails. Separate directing an AI revision from personally writing, drawing or coding the replacement. “Founder edits” cannot conceal substantial manual production.
- **Quality:** the observable standard for that particular output and who can judge it. Code running, text existing or images looking polished does not establish accuracy, humor, usability or customer value. Where a sample is needed, define the smallest representative check for the separate production workflow; record that it has not happened.
- **Dependencies and effort:** tool access/cost, generation and rework, integration, testing, rights/terms where material, founder learning/judgment and recurring support. Existing subscriptions and willingness of other participants remain unknown unless established. Ground any specialist or manual-production dependency in the actual task; explain a conflict with the intended workflow instead of silently assuming a hire or new craft skill.

Use these findings in the recommendation. A short implementation estimate may cover the software while leaving the hardest content, checking or acquisition work unassessed. Include those dependencies before claiming a faster or easier route to earnings; if the decisive quality/production step is untested, make the ranking conditional and identify what would reverse it. Keep an earlier estimate as a dated assumption, not a supported delivery schedule. AI production changes feasibility and costs; it does not establish demand or affordable acquisition.

## Customer evidence and later tests

Scouting identifies what needs investigation. The investigator designs focused customer tests; customer-connect prepares particular conversations or offers from that work. A plan, drafted message, role-play or user selection is not an observed result.

A useful test identifies the claim, relevant participants, procedure/questions, material to show, what to record, interpretation of different outcomes, and known effort/cost. Match evidence to the claim: a complaint can establish a problem account; a positive conversation cannot establish purchase; payment does not by itself establish continued value. Choose meaningful criteria for the particular test, not universal sample counts or thresholds. Preserve returned original notes, duplicates, corrections and conflicts through the record guide.

Method references: [YC's user-interview guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/) supports asking about concrete past experiences and existing attempts; the [World Bank survey methodology](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/en/methodology) illustrates why population and sampling context matter. These are method/context resources, not evidence that a proposed business works.
