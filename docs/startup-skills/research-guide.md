# Research for startup decisions

Use this guide to decide what a finding supports, which questions deserve more research, and what still needs customer evidence. The [source catalog](source-catalog.md) supplies starting places; the [record guide](record-guide.md) owns evidence storage. Use the terms in [the glossary](../../CONTEXT.md).

## From a source to a claim

An attributable complaint supports a reported experience in its original context. Read the account, date, relevant replies, suggested workarounds and resolutions. Keep geography, customer type and product/version limits. Several articles or list entries based on the same incident remain one underlying observation. Search snippets help locate evidence; inspect the underlying source before relying on its detail, or mark the claim as unverified.

Different sources answer different questions:

| Source | What it can support | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original customer account or supplied result | A reported problem, behavior or outcome in context | Identity/accuracy where unverified, prevalence, relevance to the proposed offer |
| Vendor documentation and pricing | A documented capability, published offer or supplier input | Actual suitability, total costs, satisfaction and willingness to buy a new offer |
| Buyer-authored procurement record | A stated requirement or awarded contract, according to its stage | Supplier eligibility, accessibility and applicability outside that buyer |
| Official statistics | The measured population and quantities in the stated period | Reachable buyers and demand for this particular product |
| Idea list, investor prompt or method advice | A lead, useful terminology or research method | Every opportunity-specific problem and commercial claim |

Make each important claim traceable to supporting and contrary evidence and explain confidence in that claim. A well-supported negative finding can justify revision. Sparse coverage leaves uncertainty; source failure, a search with no relevant results, and a search not performed are distinct states. Avoid extrapolating between markets or customer groups without explaining the assumption.

## Follow-up that changes a decision

Independent discovery starts from customer tasks and problems, including practitioner communities outside startup circles. Use the relevant market's local names and languages when useful. Follow the original source from any curated lead, and search beyond lists for other problems. Explain meaningful coverage gaps such as private communities or a digitally underrepresented customer group.

For promising ideas, investigate both the proposed opportunity and plausible explanations against it: an existing feature, a solved configuration issue, a cheap manual workaround, organizational causes, switching costs or satisfied users. A vendor already offering a feature challenges the claim that it is missing; it does not prove that every possible business in that market is unattractive. An old unresolved thread calls for checking the current product and linked issue/release history.

Prioritize questions whose answers could change the next step. For example, if a complaint assumes offline use is impossible, follow product documentation and relevant practitioner replies before recommending a new offline product. If the answer depends on private usage or purchasing decisions, identify the missing customer observation instead of treating more web pages as a substitute.

Scouting needs enough depth to compare customer/problem, alternatives, payer/revenue possibilities, access routes, initial delivery/support effort, material costs and dependencies. Investigation goes deeper into offer/payment terms, buying authority/process, acquisition, onboarding, continuing value, costs/cash timing and technical/data access. Apply questions to the business model; one-time licenses, usage fees, marketplaces and services enabled by software need not use subscription measures.

## Numbers and delivery requirements

Distinguish sourced, user-supplied and hypothetical inputs. Published competitor prices are evidence about that competitor, not the price a new customer will accept. Record currency, market, date, units, billing period, plan limits and tax treatment when material. Refresh changing inputs for the decision being made. Retain original amounts and any conversion basis.

Calculations show the formula, inputs and assumptions. Useful ranges or scenarios are preferable when uncertainty warrants them; avoid invented precision or an unsupported profit ranking. Simulated revenue is not a forecast. Include founder work, startup and recurring spend, usage-driven supplier costs, customer acquisition/support and timing where they affect the decision. An unpriced dependency remains unpriced.

Solo status makes installation, field work, specialist knowledge, integrations, capital and support obligations important facts. Explain what must be done and which estimates are assumptions. Do not silently turn those requirements into an eligibility filter or claim the founder can immediately deliver alone.

## Customer evidence and later tests

Scouting identifies what needs investigation. The investigator designs focused customer tests; customer-connect prepares particular conversations or offers from that work. A plan, drafted message, role-play or user selection is not an observed result.

A useful test identifies the claim, relevant participants, procedure/questions, material to show, what to record, interpretation of different outcomes, and known effort/cost. Match evidence to the claim: a complaint can establish a problem account; a positive conversation cannot establish purchase; payment does not by itself establish continued value. Choose meaningful criteria for the particular test, not universal sample counts or thresholds. Preserve returned original notes, duplicates, corrections and conflicts through the record guide.

Method references: [YC's user-interview guidance](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/) supports asking about concrete past experiences and existing attempts; the [World Bank survey methodology](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/en/methodology) illustrates why population and sampling context matter. These are method/context resources, not evidence that a proposed business works.
