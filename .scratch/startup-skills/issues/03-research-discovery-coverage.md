## Question

Which source families and research approaches would let a new startup scout investigate India, the US, and other markets across software, services, physical products, and operational businesses, without letting English search results or freely accessible technology communities define the opportunity space?

Use a small representative set of primary sources, such as official statistics, procurement records, first-party product/pricing information, and customer or industry records. Explain what each establishes, what it does not, geographic/language coverage, and visible access limitations. Include approaches for markets with sparse online evidence; limited access must remain a research gap, not proof that demand is absent.

The output is research for source-selection policy, not a universal source catalog or an API implementation. Do not repeat historical access claims from the old scripts as current facts. Identify which gaps genuinely need human observations or later research.

## Answer

Resolved by: discovery-coverage-research

Research/access date: 2026-09-30. This is a bounded source-coverage investigation, not a startup shortlist or validation exercise. Eight representative primary reference pages support the findings below; linked interfaces illustrate access limitations. No accounts, outreach, purchases, or data integrations were created.

### Findings

Scouting needs several source families chosen around a customer population and a claim. No inspected source represents the entire opportunity space. Official population estimates, reported business constraints, procurement requirements, trade records, and first-party offers provide different evidence. Combining them can expose opportunities that would be missed by technology communities alone; that conclusion is an interpretation of their differing coverage, not proof of demand for any proposed startup.

The limitation statements in the last column are analytical boundaries on using these sources. They are not additional claims made by their publishers. For example, moving from a supplier's public price to a proposed startup's profitability requires evidence this research did not gather.

### Proposed implications for the scout design

These are proposals for later design decisions, not settled skill requirements or new exclusion rules.

1. **Start with a population, then a source.** Name customer/user/payer, geography, urban/rural or formal/informal relevance, and business activity. Search sources capable of representing that population before interpreting search-result volume. An open brief can sample contrasting populations without committing the user to them.
2. **Use more than one kind of evidence where the claim needs it.** Population statistics can establish a plausible pool; buyer records can reveal requirements; first-party alternatives and supplier information can show existing behavior or cost structure. None replaces a customer's demonstrated problem or purchase behavior. A copied press release on several sites remains one underlying source.
3. **Record coverage explicitly.** For each material finding, retain URL, publisher, access date, measurement period, geography, population, units/classification, original language, translation status, access state, and the claim supported. Keep the fields compact and permit unknowns. Record whether a source was fully read, only indexed, or inaccessible.
4. **Separate missing evidence from negative evidence.** “No accessible result found using these searches” is a coverage observation. A relevant buyer saying they do not experience the problem, or a failed test with a suitable population, would be contrary evidence of a different kind. Sparse sources should produce uncertainty and a specific next step rather than an automatic rejection or fabricated market estimate.
5. **Check languages and local terminology.** Search local names for the workflow, occupation, product, and current workaround, with regional/native-language variants where relevant. Preserve original terms and label translations. Do not assume Hindi covers India or English covers a country because its statistics are published in English. Native-speaker verification becomes a gap when meaning affects the hypothesis.
6. **Make breadth inspectable without forced quotas.** Summarize what geographies, sectors, business types, and customer populations were considered and where coverage was weak. The user can see when the current results skew toward online software communities. A fixed requirement to output one idea per country or business type would manufacture diversity without evidence.
7. **Expose feasibility separately.** Working capital, licensing, operational effort, supplier eligibility, and customer access can be research needs or observed constraints. They should not silently become software-only, US-only, or solo-only selection rules. Concrete adverse evidence still belongs in the assessment.

### Sparse-online-evidence path and remaining gaps

For an underrepresented population, a useful next research step is to identify the relevant local statistical office, procurement body, trade body, distributor, or field setting, then find customer-owned records or observations of the actual workflow. Proposed evidence could include invoices, stockout or rework logs, repair records, delivery delays, or how a business currently buys a service. These are examples of records to seek with permission, not records inspected here.

If desk research cannot reach that evidence, hand off a precise human task: which customer role and location to observe, which recent incident to ask about, which document would substantiate it, and what ambiguity remains. Interviews, site visits, regional-language interpretation, restricted trade-group access, and actual quotes may need a human or a later authorized research effort. This ticket does not authorize contacting anyone.

The main unresolved gaps are idea-specific rather than a need for a larger universal catalog: the exact customer population and language; reachable sources for agriculture, household consumers, or specialist sectors outside these examples; current local prices and supplier terms; the accessibility of restricted records; and whether observed problems lead to paid demand. The ASUSE full-report URL returned a tool error, so the accessible official release/endnote was used instead. No source failure was counted as evidence that a market lacks demand.

Resolution scope: the source families, coverage pitfalls, and sparse-evidence approach are now specified well enough to inform the scout design. Whether to adopt particular coverage fields, search defaults, or output behavior remains for the later human decision tickets.
