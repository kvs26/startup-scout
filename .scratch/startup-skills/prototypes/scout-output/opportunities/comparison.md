# Three ideas: what they do and how they compare

**FICTIONAL EXAMPLE.** These products, accounts and situations are made up to demonstrate the report. No real research or customer contact was performed. Usage examples explain proposed products; they are not observed customer behavior.

## Understand the ideas first

### 1. Help small US agencies get overdue invoices paid

**The idea:** software for small companies that do client work, such as website design. It would show which bills are overdue, track why payment is stuck, and help staff follow up.

**Example use:** a client has not paid because they say part of a website is unfinished. The agency owner opens the tool, sees the disputed work and previous messages, and records who needs to act next. Sending another payment reminder alone would not settle that disagreement.

**Why consider it?** In the fictional accounts, agency owners describe sending repeated emails about unpaid bills. But one later says their existing billing software already handles reminders. A new product would need to help with something still unresolved, such as tracking payment disputes. We have not established that it can do this better or that anyone would pay for it. [Accounts and limits](agency-invoice-follow-up/research.md).

### 2. Help factory workers pass repair information to the next shift

**The idea:** a shared repair log for small Indian factories. Workers would record what went wrong with a machine, what was tried, and what still needs doing, so the next shift can pick up the work.

**Example use:** the evening shift notices a machine overheating and records the checks already performed. The morning shift reads the note instead of starting the same checks again. This is an imagined use, not a measured saving.

**Why consider it?** One fictional supervisor describes missing repair notes. Another says a signed paper log works well. The product would need a clear benefit over paper, messages or existing maintenance software. [Accounts and limits](factory-shift-handovers/research.md).

### 3. Help museums show captions when the internet is unreliable

**The idea:** software for small UK museums to prepare, store and display captions for exhibit videos on devices without needing an internet connection during playback.

**Example use:** a visitor watches an exhibit video with captions even when the museum's connection drops. Staff prepare and check the captions beforehand. This is an illustrative product proposal, not a confirmed museum requirement.

**Why consider it?** A fictional accessibility discussion mentions trouble with captions without a connection, but it does not come from a museum buyer. Preparing captions in advance with existing software might already solve the whole problem. We first need to establish why a museum would need another product. [Accounts and limits](museum-offline-captions/research.md).

## Compare them side by side

All entries below refer to the fictional example. Payment models and delivery needs are proposals to check.

| What supports the problem? | Accounts directly describe chasing bills, although existing reminders help one agency. | A supervisor describes lost notes, but another is satisfied with paper. | Only an indirect discussion; no museum buyer describes needing this product. |

No customer access, acceptable price or willingness to pay is established for any of the three. These examples do not support a revenue, profit or cheapest-to-build ranking.

## What this comparison means

**I would investigate the agency idea first, while checking whether existing software makes it unnecessary.** Compared with the museum idea, its fictional accounts are closer to the actual customer and task. Compared with the factory idea, the proposed product appears to need less work at customer sites. That is a delivery assumption, not a proven cost advantage. The strongest objection is also clear: reminders already exist, and we do not yet know whether handling disputes leaves room for a useful new product.

**The factory idea has a clearer customer problem to investigate than the museum idea, but appears more demanding to deliver alone than the agency tool.** Lost repair notes give us a specific workflow to examine. Site visits, device testing and support could add work. Paper working well elsewhere is a reason to check which factories actually need a change. If you already had access to a factory willing to show its process, this could become the better next investigation; we have not assumed that access.

**The museum idea currently has the weakest basis for further investment.** Unlike the other two, it lacks an account from the proposed customer describing this problem. Before designing features or estimating income, find out whether museum staff have a problem existing caption tools do not solve. Less evidence means less certainty, not proof that the idea is bad.

**Your next choice:** explore the agency's unresolved payment work, inspect a factory's repair-note process, or first establish whether museums need another caption tool. The recommendation is about which question to investigate next; you choose the idea.
