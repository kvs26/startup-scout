# Help small agencies follow up on disputed invoices

**Fictional prototype.** The agencies, billing product and accounts below are invented to test how the scout explains an idea. This is not research into a real business.

Imagine a small US agency that has finished a website project and sent its client an invoice. The due date passes. The owner sends a reminder, but the client says some of the work was never approved. The owner now has to find the agreement and earlier messages, work out who needs to answer the client, and settle the disagreement before asking for payment again. Another automatic reminder would not do that work. This is an imagined situation, not an observed customer case.

## The idea

The possible product is software that helps an agency keep track of what is holding up each unpaid invoice. In the example above, the owner would record the client's objection beside the invoice, attach or link the relevant messages, and give a colleague the next action. The agency would still decide whether the client's complaint is valid, what work it owes, and what to say. The software would make the status and next step visible to the team; it would not settle the dispute for them.

A narrow first version could let staff enter this information themselves. Connecting to a billing system might save entry later, but the access needed and the cost of maintaining that connection have not been checked. We have not tested either version with an agency.

The proposed buyer is an agency owner, paying a monthly subscription for the team to track these invoices. No price has been set, and no owner has said they would pay. The product would need to be useful each time an invoice gets stuck; how often that happens is also unknown.

## Why investigate it?

Two invented agency accounts describe work around overdue invoices. One owner reports repeated reminder emails ([E1](research.md#e1--manual-chasing)). Another says payment is delayed when a client disputes the work or its approval ([E2](research.md#e2--disputed-invoices)). These examples point to different problems. If a client simply forgot to pay, a reminder may help. If the client disagrees about the work, someone has to resolve that disagreement.

The fictional billing product LedgerNest already advertises automatic reminders ([E3](research.md#e3--incumbent-reminder-capability)). In a later account, the owner from E1 says those reminders reduced their manual messages, while disputes remained ([E4](research.md#e4--newer-account-of-existing-reminders)). E4 is an update from the same invented agency, not a new customer. It weakens the case for a general reminder tool and gives us a more specific question: is managing disputed invoices painful enough to need another product?

An agency might already handle this with its billing software, email and a shared spreadsheet. We have not checked current dispute features in existing products or learned why an agency would switch. The fictional accounts show neither how common disputes are nor whether agencies would buy this proposed software. [The detailed claim assessment](research.md#claim-assessment) keeps those limits visible.

## What it would take to try

Public agency communities and company contact pages are possible ways to find owners who can explain their process. They are leads, not established customer access. The useful first conversation would walk through a real, anonymized overdue invoice: what caused the delay, where the agreement and messages live, who decides the next step, and which parts their current software already handles.

To build a useful version, the founder would need to understand the path from agreed work to client approval, invoice, objection and payment. That basic understanding is needed before designing a prototype. Reliable paid delivery would also require checking how teams handle exceptions and protecting invoice and client information. A practical learning exercise is to follow one disputed invoice with an experienced agency owner or bookkeeper and explain every handoff afterward. The need for specialist review remains unassessed.

Manual entry could avoid an initial integration, but it would create extra work for agency staff. A connected version would need billing-system permissions and ongoing maintenance. Hosting, API charges, onboarding, support and the time spent resolving customer problems are still unpriced. There is no basis here for a launch-cost or profit estimate.

This US example also sits outside the founder's recorded India-first market preference. Their software background may help build a prototype, but agency billing knowledge and customer access are unassessed. Their usual 10–19 hours a week and roughly ₹5,000 monthly starting budget make the unknown integration and support work important to check before treating the idea as a fit.

## Recommendation and next question

Investigate the narrower dispute problem before proposing a product. It remains possible that existing billing tools and a simple shared process already solve it. First check current alternatives, then ask agency owners with recent disputes to show what remained unresolved after reminders were enabled. A purchase decision would require separate evidence. No user selection is recorded.
