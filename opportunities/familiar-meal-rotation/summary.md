# A week of dinners from meals you already cook

A household cook may know twenty meals everyone likes and still struggle to decide what fits this week. We would sell a small web planner that turns that familiar repertoire into complete dinners, accounting for busy evenings, usual side dishes and repetition preferences. The customer operates the planner and supplies the food knowledge. The proposed advantage is less setup and weekly correction than a recipe app or a manually maintained rotation; it is not yet demonstrated.

**Imagined example:** someone enters twenty meal names, their usual sides and rough effort, then marks Tuesday and Thursday as busy. The planner proposes five dinners, a planned-leftovers evening and an open evening. They replace one dinner and export the week. Their recipes can stay wherever they already keep them.

The initial price hypothesis is **₹499 once per household for the current planner version**, including reusable rotations and export. Support and update terms must be defined before selling. The offer does not assume unlimited personal setup or a permanent cloud-service promise. Repeated use comes from new weeks; repeated revenue initially needs new customers.

## The customer and what they want

Start with cooks who already maintain a familiar repertoire but spend time choosing complete meals. This is narrower than everyone who struggles with food decisions. They may currently use a paper list, a recipe organizer, a fixed weekly cycle or a general AI tool. The founder's personal meal indecision prompted the search, but its cause and similarity to this customer's situation remain unknown.

An existing Recipe Keeper user describes building a separate AppSheet system for repetition, seasonality, side dishes and limits on new recipes. That is concrete work on the proposed job. Another discussion describes personal-library users creating randomizers or exporting recipes into AI. These are public self-reports, not representative research or buying commitments. See [the original workaround](../../scouting-runs/2026-10-18-discovery-menu.md#i002-e001) and [the recent selection discussion](../../scouting-runs/2026-10-18-discovery-menu.md#i002-e003).

## What the first version would do

The proposed input is meal names, usual sides, broad effort and a few household rules. The output is an editable week with understandable reasons for choices and a clear explanation when rules cannot all be satisfied. The customer approves the meals and decides what they can actually cook. A name/link-based start could avoid importing a whole recipe collection; whether it is meaningfully easier is a test question.

The initial product would organize user-supplied familiar meals. Supplying new recipes, current grocery prices, clinical nutrition advice or delivery integrations would create different quality and data obligations. Preserving edits, exporting the repertoire and replacing a dinner without unwanted repetitions are essential reliability checks. Nothing has been implemented or trialled.

## Why someone might buy it and keep using it

The household cook is the proposed user and payer. A small one-time purchase might be attractive if the first usable week takes little effort and later weeks need fewer decisions. Historical accounts report paying for Paprika and Plan to Eat, demonstrating some spending in the broader category. They do not establish Indian willingness to pay ₹499, payment for this narrower tool, retention or affordable acquisition. [Category-purchase evidence](../../scouting-runs/2026-10-18-discovery-menu.md#i002-e002).

New households, referrals and later licensed creator repertoires could produce more sales. Shared-household features or other recurring services are possible expansion hypotheses. A low-price utility is not automatically a business capable of replacing income; distribution, support and a valuable expansion must be demonstrated.

## Evidence and existing alternatives

The strongest support is people spending effort on specific meal-selection workarounds. The strongest contrary finding is that **automatic rotation from personal recipes already exists for free**. Amamable advertises automatic 1–14-day plans, swaps and shopping lists. OnRotation offers free familiar-meal planning with complexity and last-cooked tracking; its mobile apps are at different development stages. RecipeSage provides free recurring calendar entries. These pages were read on 2026-10-18; products were not installed. [Direct competitor evidence](../../scouting-runs/2026-10-18-discovery-menu.md#i002-e004).

Paprika's US iOS listing was $4.99; Plan to Eat listed $5.95/month or $49/year; CuliPlan offers free core planning. Platform and regional prices vary. A fixed paper rotation and general AI are serious alternatives. One user in the recent Paprika discussion reported that an exported collection plus ChatGPT worked well. [Current broader supply](../../scouting-runs/2026-10-18-discovery-menu.md#shared-e002).

Our proposed reason to be chosen is **a useful complete-dinner plan from fewer setup details, with fewer later corrections**. That could matter to a less technical cook, but we have not established that competing products require more work. If Amamable, OnRotation or paper work equally well, the proposed paid distinction fails. Convenience must include the effort of entering and maintaining information.

## How to find the first customers

Recipe-organization communities contain people describing this exact work. A possible route is a permitted demonstration showing a real household's full setup and the resulting week, followed by a cooking or meal-planning creator demonstrating their own authorized repertoire. An interested cook would compare a limited free example before considering the paid version.

These are public routes, not confirmed access. Community permission, creator willingness, conversion, acquisition expense and a first target geography remain unknown. No one was contacted. Early English-language evidence cannot establish a global or India-wide market.

## What building and running it would require

The founder would direct implementation, learn household planning, specify rules, inspect exceptions and handle support. AI can assist implementation; it does not establish that rules, saved edits or exports work reliably. Data are supplied by the household, reducing the need for a recipe corpus or retailer integration. A simple first version need not call a model during planning. Household synchronization, automated imports and recipe packs would add support and rights work if later included.

Startup work includes observing actual planning, testing input requirements, implementation, reliability checks and clear onboarding. Domain, production tools and independent technical review are unpriced. Ongoing work includes hosting, updates, customer acquisition, refunds and support. Supplier prices are inputs only: Cloudflare offers a limited free tier and paid Workers from US$5/month; storage/workload costs may be additional. ## Domain knowledge needed

Before conversations, learn how a dish becomes a complete dinner, what sides people consider compatible, how shopping days affect choices, and when repetition or leftovers are welcome. A saved recipe is not necessarily something the household actually cooks. These distinctions determine whether a suggested week feels usable.

Before paid delivery, learn how changed attendance, skipped meals, effort limits and conflicting preferences should affect the plan. The key quality judgment is whether cooks accept the dinners with fewer corrections. Your practical meal-planning expertise is unassessed. Organizing familiar meals does not inherently require a nutrition qualification; supplying clinical or safety judgments would require different expertise and review.

Start by reconstructing two recent weeks with a household cook. Record the familiar complete meals and explain each deviation from the plan. Compare a fixed rotation and a free planner using the same meals. You should be able to explain why a dinner fits an evening and which facts the product cannot infer. No reliable learning-time estimate exists yet.

## Research limits

This is public desk research from 2026-10-18. Sources include selective, unverified English-language customer accounts, historical category purchases and current vendor documentation. No product was benchmarked. The user's own recent meal decision remains unexplained. No accepted price, buying result, retention, reliable delivery cost or profitable acquisition channel is established.

The scout round stopped after fifty complete assessments and comparison because the remaining decisive questions require workflow comparisons and actual buying behavior. Competition narrowed the hypothesis; it did not establish either inevitable failure or profitable demand.
