# Help Indian research teams resolve questionable survey answers

The hard part often begins after a survey answer is flagged. A researcher can spot a repeated household number or an unusual value in the day's data, but the record alone cannot explain what happened. Someone has to ask the field team, check the answer against the interview or permitted notes, and decide whether to correct it. If that conversation is scattered across a spreadsheet and messages, it can be hard to see which questions are still open and who owes the next answer.

**The proposed business** is an online workspace for smaller Indian social, education and development research teams that already make lists of these questionable records. A coordinator would import the list, send each case to a field supervisor, collect an explanation, and pass it to the research lead for a decision. The software would keep that history together and export the decisions for the team's usual process of correcting survey data. It would organize the follow-up; the researchers would still decide what each answer means and whether it should change.

**Imagined example:** An education survey's daily file contains a household identifier that appears twice, a blank answer and an unusually high school-fee amount. The coordinator imports those three cases using study codes rather than names or phone numbers. A supervisor checks with the interviewers and explains that one identifier was mistyped, the blank answer was a permitted skip, and the fee amount was real. The research lead approves those conclusions. The team exports the explanations and the one approved correction, while any unanswered cases remain open. When tomorrow's file arrives, the workspace keeps today's decisions instead of making the team start again. This example explains the proposed workflow; it is not a customer result.

The proposed buyer is the research organization or survey firm, paying for access during each active study-month. A separate setup fee could cover mapping its issue file and training staff. No price or payment terms have been agreed, and no buyer has said they would pay. The reason to investigate the idea is a real coordination task; the reason for caution is that free and already-licensed tools may handle it well enough.

## The work behind the idea

An *enumerator* interviews participants and records their answers. During fieldwork, a research assistant or data manager checks incoming records, often with a spreadsheet, a script, or a feature in the survey platform. A flag means “look at this,” not “change this.” A field supervisor may ask the interviewer what happened or arrange a permitted follow-up. A research lead then decides whether the answer is valid, needs correction, or needs more investigation. The person updating the dataset must be able to see that decision and its reason.

Indian sources show that this work happens. [Outline India describes daily checks and review by supervisors and researchers](research.md#e002). An [Indus Action education survey describes weekly checks during eight weeks of phone fieldwork](research.md#e003). An [IPA request for survey services includes daily quality reporting](research.md#e004). These sources establish a workflow and its roles. They do not tell us that smaller teams dislike their current software or would buy another product.

The clearest account of follow-up trouble comes from an [older household survey in Madhya Pradesh](research.md#e001). Its team tracked errors in a spreadsheet and discussed them in meetings and messages; staff found automated notifications helpful because replies and corrections could be missed. The study introduced its system late, as staff were also becoming more familiar with the work. Its before-and-after results therefore cannot tell us how much the notifications caused the improvement. It is a reason to ask today's teams about lost or slow handoffs, not evidence that they need this particular product.

The first teams worth speaking with would be those already generating issue lists but still chasing supervisors for explanations. A team with few unresolved cases, or one satisfied with its current process, may have no reason to change. How often the problem occurs in smaller Indian teams is unknown.

## What the first version would do

The team would keep its survey-collection platform and its existing rules for spotting possible errors. The first version would take one agreed CSV file, a spreadsheet-like table of flagged records. During setup, the coordinator would show which columns identify the study, the submitted interview, the question, the concern and the supervisor responsible. A *questionnaire* lists what was asked; a *codebook* explains coded answers. The workspace would need enough of that context to show a case clearly, but it would not need the full set of participants' answers.

Before accepting an import, the coordinator would preview the cases and confirm the column mapping. This matters because a mistyped or changed identifier could attach an old decision to the wrong interview. The software would keep the import date and source, preserve identifiers with leading zeroes, and flag uncertain matches or changed columns. A missing case in an incomplete file would stay open rather than silently count as resolved.

A supervisor would see assigned cases on a phone-friendly page and add an explanation or a reference to supporting material. The coordinator could give the team a task list through a communication channel the customer already permits. The research lead would record the decision: accept the answer, approve a correction, request more information, or leave the case unresolved. A supervisor's explanation would not itself change the survey data. The team would export approved decisions, apply any corrections in its existing process, and confirm what was actually applied. Later imports would preserve the earlier history; a new study wave, or round of interviews, might need a fresh review if the question or checking rule changed.

That is a proposed design, not a tested product. We do not know whether its imports would match real files easily, whether supervisors would use it, or whether it would save enough work to justify another tool. The initial offer would stay with one customer's issue-list format and workflow. Automatic platform connections, custom scientific checks and automatic messaging would require separate evidence and scope.

## Why buying it is uncertain

The most direct alternative is a well-kept spreadsheet plus the team's existing messages. [IPA even publishes a quality-action template](research.md#e016) that helps assign responsibility. A new subscription must make the handoff meaningfully easier than that simple arrangement.

Existing software is also capable. [KoboToolbox](research.md#e009) has review states, permissions and editing history; its [logs](research.md#e010) and [synchronous exports](research.md#e012) weaken any claim that these features or easy downloads are missing. The free [IPA DataSure](research.md#e007) already supports visual checks, correction history and reproducible exports, with a [documented correction workflow](research.md#e008). The free [World Bank iehfc](research.md#e005) makes some checks easier through a visual interface.

A team already using [SurveyCTO](research.md#e011) has integrated flags, comments, review and corrections; its published Basic price was US$250/month on monthly billing or US$225/month with annual billing when checked for this research. [DataQuad](research.md#e018) advertises a US$15/month Standard plan for two projects, though its runtime quality and India-specific costs were not tested. [FieldGovern](research.md#e019) is another India-facing vendor; its pages disagreed about a free tier.

The proposed opening is narrower than “find survey errors” or “keep a correction log.” It is helping several people finish the investigation of a flagged case while retaining their current checking tools. Public documentation has not shown that the products above fail at that job. An [old Kobo bulk-editing request was resolved](research.md#e020), and a [complainant who could not export data later confirmed recovery](research.md#e021); neither is a current missing-feature claim. We need to watch a real team's process before saying this workspace has an advantage.

The daily users would be a coordinator, supervisors and a research lead. A project manager, survey-firm owner or institutional budget holder might approve payment, but that path has not been checked. A monthly charge for each active study fits the months when new cases arrive. A completed study would need an export and an agreed arrangement for keeping or deleting its records. Another study might bring another purchase, but fieldwork is project-based, so continuous year-round subscriptions cannot be assumed. Setup, custom checks and research advice would each need their own scope. No purchase, renewal, paid pilot or actual product use has been observed.

## How to find the first teams

Public project and services pages can identify organizations running multi-person surveys. [MFRS India describes fieldwork and issue reporting](research.md#e014); [Outline India's technical role description](research.md#e015) shows the job language to look for. These are ways to find relevant people, not introductions or likely customers. Both organizations may already have strong internal tools or services.

A useful first conversation would reconstruct one recent case from the initial flag to the final dataset decision: where the issue list lived, who asked the supervisor, how the reply came back, and what was still troublesome after using current tools. The team could show an invented or properly permitted example file. A separate conversation with someone who approves software spending would reveal whether a purchase is even possible. No such access or conversation has been arranged.

## What building and running it would require

The application would need reliable file imports, staff permissions, case assignments, replies, approval history, exports and backups. The hardest part is keeping the right decision attached to the right record. A respondent may appear in more than one study wave; a submitted interview may be edited; a code can mean “not asked” rather than “missing.” [Kobo's identifier and version guidance](research.md#e009) shows why the customer's record keys and changes must be understood before an import is trusted. The system would also need to show conflicting replies, incomplete files and changed formats instead of quietly merging them.

A coded issue can still reveal a person when combined with other information. Before using real data, the customer would need to decide which fields the product may store, who may see them, where they may be hosted and when they must be deleted. An experienced fieldwork or data manager should review the issue and approval process. An experienced technical reviewer should check customer separation, permissions, exports, deletion, backups and restoration before confidential paid use. These are delivery needs inferred from the proposed product, not a claim of a specific legal qualification.

Support would continue after launch. Someone would have to map changed files, explain import problems, help supervisors use the page and restore data if needed. Fieldwork deadlines could make a slow response costly. We have no measured support load, so a small server bill says little about whether one founder can operate the service.

A synthetic-data prototype on an existing computer could avoid hosting costs. For a small hosted experiment, the saved research used one published supplier price and one storage assumption: a [US$7/month Lightsail instance](research.md#e017) plus 40 GB of retained snapshots at an assumed US$0.05/GB/month (US$2) equals **US$9/month** before tax and other services. At an **assumed** ₹100 per US$1 planning rate, that is ₹900/month. This is not a current exchange rate, an India bill or a production deployment quote. It would leave ₹4,100 of the founder's ₹5,000 monthly budget before a domain, monitoring, communication, accounting, additional backups, independent review and founder time. A customer requiring redundancy, particular data location or procurement checks could change the cost substantially.

Time may be tighter than cash. If two active studies each needed two support hours per week, support would take four hours, about 21–40% of the founder's usual 10–19-hour week. At four hours per study, support would take eight hours, leaving 2–11 hours for building, learning, selling and onboarding. Both support rates are examples, not observations. Build time and payment delays are also unknown. A working prototype, a safe paid deployment and a service one person can sustain are different milestones.

## Domain knowledge needed

Start with the roles and decisions in one survey team. Who spots an unusual response? Who asks the interviewer? Who can approve a correction? A supervisor's explanation, a lead's approval and an actual change to the dataset are three different events. You need this distinction before designing the screen that moves a case from one person to another.

Next learn how the team identifies a record. A respondent ID, a submitted interview ID and a study wave are not interchangeable: the same person may be interviewed again, and a submission may be edited. Match them incorrectly and the workspace may display someone else's answer or attach a decision to the wrong case. [Kobo's guidance](research.md#e009) is a starting point, but an experienced data manager should check the keys in any real customer's file.

Read a questionnaire and codebook alongside an issue list. An empty answer may have been deliberately skipped; a number may stand for refusal rather than an amount. A flag is a request to investigate, not proof of an error. Learn what evidence a supervisor can use, what the research lead accepts, and how the team records a correction so its history can be reconstructed. The [J-PAL data-quality guide](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/data-quality-checks?lang=en) and [DataSure walkthrough](https://data.poverty-action.org/data-quality/datasure/how-to-datasure.html) provide introductory examples. Before paid use, the customer's responsible staff must also settle what data may be stored and who may see it. Your familiarity with these research practices is currently unassessed.

A practical first exercise uses synthetic household-survey data: create one mistyped identifier, one person interviewed in two waves, one permitted blank, one real unusual value and one confirmed error. Write the issue list, supervisor reply and researcher's decision for each. Import a second day's list and check that the decisions survive without cases being merged incorrectly. Ask a fieldwork practitioner to challenge your interpretation. You should then be able to explain why each case is still open, accepted, or approved for correction and show how the export supports that decision. Access to such a reviewer and the time needed to reach this point remain unknown.

## What happens next

**Recommendation:** Investigate a current team's handoff before building. Indian sources show that survey quality review and supervisor follow-up are real work, and one older study shows that communication can fail. The commercial case is weak so far because DataSure, Kobo, SurveyCTO and an orderly spreadsheet may already do enough.

The decision-changing question is whether a smaller Indian team still spends substantial effort resolving flagged cases **after using or seriously considering those alternatives**. Ask a coordinator to walk through a recent unresolved case, then ask a budget owner how a purchase of this specific tool would be approved and paid. Continue only if the workflow reveals a recurring weakness the proposed product can address and a realistic buying path. If a better spreadsheet or configuration fixes it, leave the idea for now.
