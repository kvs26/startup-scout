# Assess a business that could make money

Use for every candidate and at the end of a round. Assess the customer's reason to choose, ability to reach them and economics together. A worthwhile test can involve a competitive product, a discretionary purchase or a new category. Social impact and novelty are optional attributes. Assess lawful businesses within applicable safety constraints; entertainment and companionship need no productivity justification.

## Small products and speed to revenue

When the user selects small products aimed at earning quickly, **earning money as quickly as credibly possible is the top priority**. Compare opportunities primarily by the path to first revenue within the founder's actual time and budget. These are products the founder owns and sells, such as a narrow utility, a playful/novelty app or a timely trend-based product. Client freelancing is an eligible separate category, included when selected individually or through an all-categories search.

- Assess the whole path: minimum sellable version, learning/build/setup effort, launch or platform approval, reachable first audience, promotion effort/cost, payment conversion and payout delay. Estimate a supported range or relative ordering, identifying assumptions and blockers; leave timing unknown where evidence cannot support it. A short build is not evidence of a quick first sale.
- State who pays, what payment buys and how the first sale or other monetization event could occur. Compare first revenue with positive contribution, recovery of startup/acquisition costs and cash actually received. An early sale with larger costs does not establish earnings. Check dependencies such as ad eligibility, traffic thresholds or marketplace review when they control timing.
- For playful, gimmicky or potentially viral products, examine the hook, why users might share, the first reachable audience/channel, a concrete promotion experiment, conversion to payment and costs if traffic spikes. Treat virality as an uncertain acquisition hypothesis; explain whether modest reach could pay and whether the opportunity depends on a short-lived trend. Views and downloads alone do not establish revenue.
- For this track, a credible opportunity to earn sooner can outrank a slower, more durable or larger business. Report limited lifespan, copying and weak retention as trade-offs rather than automatic rejection reasons. Keep truthful product claims, viable costs and substantive evidence; speed is a comparison priority, not a guaranteed income deadline or permission to skip the research-depth requirements.

## Freelancing and paid client work

Research paid assignments performed for clients within the scout's software/AI/similar-technology scope. Explain the buyer, concrete deliverable, usable skills and learning needed, proof of ability, route to the first client, sales effort, delivery/revisions/support, fees and payment/payout terms. Compare likely time to a first paid assignment and net earnings after costs and founder hours using evidence or explicit assumptions. An advertised freelance rate or marketplace listing is not a secured client or guaranteed income.

Keep client work distinct from revenue earned by selling an owned product. A repeatable packaged service, agency or productization path may follow; explain what changes operationally rather than counting the same proposition again under a new label. Apply the brief's earning priority and preserve the user's planned AI-assisted production and personal client responsibilities. Scouting researches these opportunities; accepting assignments or contacting clients remains outside its execution scope.

## Balance entry and ambition

Use the founder's selected search priority to assess both the first sellable offer and the business it could become. Longer-term startup searches can accept a slower start for a stronger durable business or growth path; the small-product track follows the speed-to-revenue priority above. Explain how profit could grow through more customers, greater value per customer, repeat purchases or delivery that scales beyond founder hours, and what would need to become true. Ease of building or a quick first sale alone does not establish viable economics. A simple or inexpensive product can still have strong quality and upside; a complex or premium product still needs a credible commercial case. Current resources shape the starting scope, not an assumed ceiling on ambition.

## Why this version could be chosen

Compare the strongest realistic alternative at the target customer's tier and use pattern. Verify current capabilities, prices, plan limits, setup and switching effort. Include positive customer accounts so the comparison explains what incumbents do well.

Pick the relevant competitive parameters and make the proposed difference falsifiable:

| Parameter | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Total cost or price | What does the customer pay in money and effort, at comparable usage? What structural cost or scope difference could sustain our price? |
| Value or quality | Which result or experience improves, for whom, and how would someone judge it? |
| Simplicity or speed | Which setup steps, waiting time or repeated effort disappear? What does the narrower scope give up? |
| Audience or localization | Which specific language, taste, profession or use occasion is served better? Is that audience reachable? |
| Distribution or service | What channel, relationship, onboarding or delivery experience helps win customers? What is actually accessible to this founder? |
| Packaging, integration or trust | What combination, compatibility, deployment or assurance changes the buying choice? What work does it impose on us? |

For each material advantage state the existing baseline, proposed change, supporting evidence, remaining assumption and a practical comparison that could disprove it. A testable, research-grounded hypothesis can qualify; a demonstrated win is not required before proposing investigation. Branding and experience can matter, but require an audience-specific account of their appeal and a way to test it. New customers can buy without switching, and entertainment purchases can coexist with competing products.

Competitors show that an offer exists; credible customer evidence may also show spending in the category. Evaluate how another provider could reach and serve a share of those buyers without assuming it must displace the whole incumbent. A functional invention is optional. Reach, positioning, execution, quality, experience and cost can support a commercial hypothesis. A large consumer population alone does not establish affordable acquisition or profit; investigate the reachable segment and economics instead of using either crowding or market size as a shortcut.

“Cheaper because we use AI,” “better UX,” “huge market” and “capture 1%” are incomplete commercial arguments. Identify the cost mechanism, experience difference or acquisition path. If incumbents can copy the offer, explain what that means for first sales and continued differentiation, including how the advantage could strengthen. A permanent moat need not exist at the first version.

## Evidence appropriate to the motive

Business-work evidence may show a failure, time spent or budget. Consumer evidence may show enjoyment, attachment, identity, habit or convenience. Established-market evidence can support spending on a category even when no unresolved functional problem exists. Separate:

- A product exists; it has a listed price; someone reports buying it; independently checkable payments exist.
- Downloads, ratings, followers and attention; paid conversion; repeat use; renewal; profit.
- Vendor-reported revenue, third-party estimates and independently supported figures.
- Demand for an incumbent and the untested reason someone would choose our offer.

Use original material and inspect its context. Public customer behavior or credible spending evidence plus current alternatives can support a candidate without a complaint. A vendor listing or social revenue claim alone leaves a lead thin. Do not invent a psychological deficit to justify a product people enjoy. Keep current factual, legal, platform and supplier dependencies verified when material; no blanket legal assurance follows from a category label.

## Acquisition and money

Explain a concrete route to the first customers: who visits or uses the channel, what we could offer there, likely purchase steps, and what access, trust, labor or cash is missing. A directory is a route, not a relationship; a marketplace listing is not traffic; viral reach and SEO are hypotheses until supported.

Describe the payment unit and what it buys. For recurring models ask what brings repeat use and renewal; for one-time products ask about replenishing the flow of buyers, repeat purchases, upgrades or referrals. Include the first useful version and a plausible later expansion without confusing a growth story with evidence.

Use a small economic scenario when inputs allow it. Label each input as sourced, user-supplied or hypothetical; state currency, units, period, calculation and exclusions. For example:

- Contribution per paid unit = net revenue after discounts, refunds and applicable fees/taxes − variable delivery, model/API, hosting, fulfillment and support costs attributable to that unit. Avoid counting a fee twice.
- Cash remaining in a period = total contribution − fixed operating spend − acquisition cash spend. Show startup outlays separately and identify unpaid founder work; cash remaining before that labor is not full economic profit.
- Break-even paid units = fixed costs / contribution per unit, only for positive contribution and consistent periods; explain acquisition and labor excluded from this simplified figure.

Use low/high usage or support scenarios if these could reverse the result. A lower price needs a viable cost basis. Cheap software can still be expensive to acquire customers for or support. Unknown conversion, acquisition cost, churn and payment acceptance remain unknown; a scenario is not a revenue forecast. Current budget and time constrain the proposed entry, not the size of the eventual ambition.

## AI-assisted production

Assess the production plan stated in the founder profile. This founder intends to use separate skills or automations to create code, interfaces, artwork, animation, writing, audio and other digital resources with AI, while directing the work and handling customer conversations and business decisions. This is founder context, not a production task for the scout. Lack of manual coding, drawing, narration or writing experience alone is not a delivery disqualification. Identify what the founder needs to understand to specify and judge the result.

Assess a plausible creation workflow and its consequential unknowns: consistency across assets, editing and retries, integration, debugging, factual or specialist accuracy, rights/terms and customer acceptance where relevant. Where quality needs a representative output, recommend a check for the separate production workflow or assess supplied results; creating that output is outside the scout's scope. Neither AI availability nor a polished draft proves reliable paid delivery. AI used to build the business need not be an AI feature sold to customers.

Budget for tool subscriptions or API credits, generation/rework, hosting, testing and founder direction/support time. Existing paid-tool access is unknown unless recorded. Prefer an AI-assisted baseline over assumed staff or commissions; label any conventional-production scenario as an optional comparison. Identify a human specialist dependency only when grounded in the actual task, explain its role and cost, and flag any conflict with the intended workflow rather than silently assuming a hire. AI production changes the delivery assessment, not the evidence of demand, differentiation or affordable acquisition.

## Compare the researched pool

For open discovery, compare all **at least 50 completed deep assessments** before making the final recommendation. Use the discovery guide's depth audit and actual completed count. A request for one winning idea does not authorize comparing only the first few. Save a row for every researched idea with links to its assessment and reasons for its relative position; retain partial and screened ideas separately, outside the completed count.

Compare customer spending/motive, the reason to choose our offer, acquisition feasibility, economics and founder work, delivery/learning burden, product quality, growth potential and strength of evidence. Explain trade-offs in ordinary language. Distinguish a weak business case from an attractive but uncertain one. Missing private buying evidence need not put every idea below a familiar, well-documented category. Preserve uncertainty and avoid an invented overall score or unsupported revenue/profit ranking.

Apply the selected commercial priority explicitly: for small products aimed at earning quickly, use the [speed-to-revenue comparison](#small-products-and-speed-to-revenue) before durability or scale. In a deliberately mixed search, explain the different priorities of quick-earning products and longer-term startups instead of silently ranking everything by startup upside.

Recommend the strongest option for this founder under stated assumptions, and explain why it leads the closest alternatives and what could reverse that judgment. Save this winner in its opportunity folder and link it in chat. Also briefly mention **3–5 other top ideas**, excluding the winner, in the final chat response. Give each one or two sentences covering what it sells, to whom, its commercial appeal and the main doubt or reason it ranked lower. Retain their assessments in the scouting records; being a runner-up or receiving a positive per-idea verdict does not create an opportunity folder. This is comparative judgment about what to investigate, not proof it is the best possible business in the world. Keep the user’s selection separate.

If no idea merits a positive recommendation, **present the top 3–5 researched options anyway**, both in the report and the final explanation. For each, give the customer, offer and payment model; the strongest appeal; why the scout hesitates; what could make it work; and the next useful check. Label these as discussion options with their actual verdicts and keep them in scouting records until the user selects one. Explain why they lead the pool without promoting them to validated or recommended opportunities. The user can select one despite the scout's doubts. If an interrupted or explicitly smaller exercise has fewer than three researched options, present those available and state the agreed scope and actual completion status rather than inventing entries.

## Decision at the end of the round

Apply these business verdicts to each idea separately from whether its research is complete. An open campaign's final result also requires the minimum completed count and full comparison above.

**Candidate worth investigating:** evidence supports a specific customer motive, behavior or spending context; current alternatives have been checked; a concrete reason to choose our version is grounded in that research and explicitly testable; the payer, acquisition route, first version, main cost drivers and founder obligations can be explained. Residual uncertainty about buying, retention or the advantage is expected. Save the assessment and propose the next decision-changing check. In open discovery, only the final winner or a user-selected idea is promoted into an opportunity folder. This verdict does not declare demand validated or recommend a full build by default.

**Promising lead, evidence incomplete:** the commercial hypothesis is coherent, but a consequential claim remains unsupported. Preserve its deep assessment, accessible checks and missing observation. A private buying question can remain unknown after completed desk research; a listing-only or inaccessible-source pass without substantive evidence does not meet the discovery depth standard. This is an evidence limit, not proof the market is bad. The idea can still be a discussion option.

**Not recommended on current evidence:** explain the particular commercial or founder-fit objections, what could reverse them and which uncertainty is unresolved. A researched negative assessment remains in the comparison and may still appear among the top discussion options. If none in the completed pool merits a positive recommendation, explain the shared obstacles and present the top 3–5 rather than returning only a no-candidate diagnosis. See [discovery recovery](discovery.md#recover-from-another-empty-round).

State confidence separately for the customer motive, advantage, acquisition, economics and delivery. Do not require a universal number of customer interviews or purchases, a numerical startup score, paid preorders or proof of an unsolved problem to present an initially researched candidate. The 50-idea requirement sets research breadth and depth, not a positive-verdict quota. Returning the requested discussion options does not require endorsing them.
